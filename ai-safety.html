What these topics mean

These four topics overlap but are not interchangeable. AI safety asks whether the system's behavior is acceptably safe; security asks whether people or systems can misuse, manipulate, or compromise it. Both must be designed, tested, and operated throughout the lifecycle.

AI Safety

Prevent or reduce foreseeable harm from intended and unintended AI behavior. Define the allowed use, affected people, prohibited outcomes, human oversight, escalation path, and a way to contest consequential decisions. Safety controls include scoped use, representative evaluations, refusal and escalation behavior, monitoring, and rollback.

Large Language Model (LLM) Security

Protect the model, prompts, tools, data, and surrounding application from abuse. Risks include prompt injection (malicious instructions in user input or retrieved content), disclosure of sensitive data, unsafe output handling, poisoned knowledge sources, excessive tool permissions, and denial-of-wallet attacks (usage that drives unexpected model costs).

AI System Design

Design the whole system—not just the model—including user identity, data access, retrieval, model selection, tools, policy checks, user experience, observability, and fallback behavior. Prefer the least complex approach that meets the need; use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) when answers must be grounded in controlled, current enterprise information.

Production Deployment

Operate a versioned, monitored, recoverable service with an approved data flow, access controls, release gates, capacity limits, incident response, and ownership. Evaluate models and prompts before release, canary changes, track quality and safety signals, and retain a tested rollback path.

AI Governance

Assign owners for intended use, risk acceptance, model and data provenance, evaluation, privacy, and ongoing review. Record limitations and changes so that users and operators know what the system can and cannot reliably do.

Shared responsibility

The cloud platform secures its managed service; the customer remains responsible for application code, identity, data permissions, model configuration, prompts, integrations, and business decisions. The exact boundary varies by service and configuration.

Important: A system prompt, a content filter, or a model refusal is not an authorization boundary. Enforce access, validation, and policy in ordinary application and platform controls outside the model.

Standards and industry guidance

Use standards as a risk-based baseline, then map controls to applicable law, contracts, data classification, and the threat model. A framework or checklist is not itself proof of compliance or a guarantee of safety. Confirm the current edition and scope during implementation.

Recommended AI application design patterns

Identity-aware retrieval Propagate authenticated user identity and enforce document-level authorization during retrieval. Filter before content reaches the model; do not rely on the model to hide unauthorized records.
Least-privilege tools Give each tool a narrow, explicit purpose and permission scope. Require confirmation for consequential or irreversible actions. Validate tool arguments and results in application code.
Grounded, bounded responses Retrieve only necessary, approved content; label source material as untrusted instructions; ask for citations; check output schema and safety policy; offer escalation or abstention when evidence is weak.
Defense in depth Layer authentication, authorization, network isolation, content controls, input/output validation, secrets management, encryption, monitoring, rate limits, and incident response. No single layer is sufficient.
Model and prompt lifecycle Track approved model/version, prompt/template, retrieval index, and evaluation set. Review changes, test for regressions, deploy progressively, and keep a known-good rollback option.
Operational resilience Use timeouts, retries with limits, quotas, concurrency controls, circuit breakers (stop repeated calls to a failing dependency), graceful degradation, and a non-AI fallback for critical workflows.

Multimodal AI, media operations, and MLOps / LLMOps

Enterprise AI is a system of models, content, metadata, workflows, controls, and people—not a model endpoint alone. Multimodal architecture connects text, images, audio, and video to business context; MLOps and LLMOps make that system testable, governed, and supportable throughout its lifecycle.

Multimodal AI ecosystem

Models and services process one or more modalities: language models handle text, vision models interpret images or video, and speech models transcribe or generate audio. Systems can use separate specialist models or a model that accepts multiple modalities. Choose based on task quality, latency, cost, data handling, and operational constraints—not novelty.

Business value: Search and understand content that was previously difficult to index, reduce repetitive review, and give staff a unified way to find evidence across formats.

Media content operations

The repeatable process for receiving, validating, classifying, enriching, storing, publishing, updating, and retiring media assets. Treat the original asset as an immutable source; store derived transcripts, thumbnails, captions, and model-generated labels as versioned outputs linked back to that source.

Business value: Faster content discovery and reuse, clearer rights and retention handling, and fewer manual handoffs or duplicate assets.

Metadata-driven systems

Store descriptive, technical, business, and governance facts about an asset—such as owner, product, language, capture time, source, permissions, retention, quality, and processing versions—so applications can filter, route, retrieve, and audit content reliably.

Business value: Better search and personalization, enforceable access and retention rules, and measurable content quality and lineage.

MLOps

The engineering and operational practices for the machine learning lifecycle: data and feature pipelines, training or fine-tuning, experiment tracking, model registry, validation, deployment, monitoring, and retirement.

Business value: Reproducible releases, controlled risk, reliable model quality, and a clear path to revert a model or data change.

LLMOps

Operational practices for applications built with large language models: prompt and model versioning, retrieval evaluation, safety checks, tool boundaries, inference routing, token and cost management, traceability, and production feedback. LLMOps extends MLOps; it does not replace software, security, or data operations.

Business value: Faster safe iteration, visible quality and spend, and evidence that each release meets its intended use.

Architectural decision

First define the user task and its risk. Use deterministic processing for predictable extraction, specialist models for narrow perception tasks, and generative models where flexible interpretation or synthesis adds value. Keep critical business rules, permissions, and workflow transitions in governed application services.

Business value: The simplest solution that meets the need is usually easier to control, explain, operate, and fund.

How the parts fit together

A practical system separates content ingestion, metadata and permissions, model processing, user-facing retrieval, and the release/operations control plane. Events connect the stages; durable records and version identifiers make each result traceable.

Content and metadata contract

  • Give every asset a stable ID and record source, owner, tenant, business domain, timestamps, language, modality, classification, rights, retention, and lifecycle status.
  • Keep provenance for each derived item: source asset/version, transformation, model and prompt versions, processing time, confidence where available, and human corrections.
  • Define a governed schema and controlled vocabularies with data owners. Validate required fields, allowed values, duplicate handling, and schema evolution at ingestion.
  • Apply access and retention rules to originals, derivatives, indexes, caches, exports, and backups; deleting an asset must trigger a verifiable downstream removal workflow.

Implementation and integration approach

  1. Start with a bounded business workflow, baseline its current cycle time, error rate, cost, and user effort, then agree success and safety thresholds.
  2. Ingest through authenticated APIs or managed upload; validate format and size, scan files, verify rights and consent where applicable, and store the original in controlled object storage.
  3. Publish an asset event to a queue. Idempotent workers extract metadata, transcribe or sample media as appropriate, call approved models, validate outputs, and persist versioned derivatives and lineage.
  4. Index text and selected multimodal representations with metadata filters. Enforce user and asset permissions before returning any content to an AI model or user.
  5. Expose capabilities through business APIs and existing workflow tools. Require review for consequential labels or actions and provide a non-AI path when dependencies fail.
  6. Deploy infrastructure and services through version-controlled pipelines. Promote model, prompt, schema, and application changes only after automated checks and accountable review.

Three illustrative case studies

These are hypothetical patterns, not claims of measured results. Establish a baseline and measure actual outcomes in a pilot before committing to a business case.

Case study 1 · Media and marketing

Search and reuse across a video library

Media Operations

Editors and campaign teams need to find approved clips by topic, speaker, product, language, and usage rights. The system combines speech transcription, scene or image analysis, and business metadata; it returns source-timestamped results rather than inventing or replacing the underlying media.

Video asset
rights and owner→ Async processors
transcript and keyframes→ Catalog + search
timestamps and ACLs→ Editor workspace
preview and approval

System integration

Connect the media asset management system to an event-driven ingestion service and object store. A queue fans out transcription and visual analysis jobs; outputs are written to a metadata catalog and text/vector search index. The editor interface calls a search API that applies user, campaign, territory, and rights filters before displaying timestamped clips.

Value, benefits, and operations

Potential value includes less time spent locating footage, more reuse of cleared content, and fewer rights-related mistakes. Measure search-to-preview time, successful discovery rate, human correction rate, reuse, processing cost per asset, and rights-filter failures. Reprocess only affected assets when a model or schema changes; monitor queue lag, stale indexes, transcription quality, and asset deletion completion.

Implementation and control points

Start with a small, rights-cleared catalog and agreed metadata vocabulary. Preserve original media; store transcript, sampled-frame descriptions, and embeddings as derived artifacts with source timestamps and model versions. Have content stewards review sensitive labels and low-confidence outputs. Treat embedded instructions and uploaded captions as untrusted input; never use model-generated rights metadata as the source of authorization.

Case study 2 · Manufacturing

Multimodal quality inspection with human disposition

Industrial AI

A manufacturer wants to prioritize visual inspection of products using line-camera images and structured production context. A vision model can flag likely defects for review; a governed workflow, not an unconstrained generative model, decides whether product is held, released, or escalated.

System integration

Capture images with device, line, product, lot, and timestamp identifiers. An edge service validates image quality and buffers work during connectivity loss, then sends approved data to a central inference API or a managed edge model. Predictions, confidence, model version, and reviewer disposition are written to the quality management system through a typed API.

Value, benefits, and operations

Potential value includes faster triage, consistent inspection support, and better traceability. Measure defect-class recall and precision, false-negative risk, review time, line availability, drift by product or camera, and cost per inspected unit. Monitor image quality, device health, inference latency, class distribution, and human disagreement; define a safe manual inspection fallback.

Implementation and control points

Build a representative, approved image set with traceable labels and separate evaluation data by time, line, or product where appropriate. Validate camera calibration and input quality; evaluate critical defect classes and operating conditions before release. Deploy through staged rollout, retain a known-good model, and require qualified human disposition until risk evidence supports a different policy. Keep model outputs advisory and outside direct machinery or safety-interlock control.

Case study 3 · Financial services

Document and call evidence for a compliance review

Regulated Workflow

Reviewers need to examine authorized documents and recorded customer interactions, locate relevant evidence, and prepare a case summary. Speech-to-text, document extraction, and an LLM-based summarizer can assist, while the reviewer remains accountable for conclusions and case disposition.

System integration

Ingest from approved document and call-recording systems with case and customer identifiers. Apply retention, legal hold, consent, and role-based access rules before processing. Store transcripts and extracted fields with timestamps or page references; a retrieval service filters by case permission, and the case-management UI presents cited evidence and an editable draft.

Value, benefits, and operations

Potential value includes reduced search and drafting effort, more consistent evidence presentation, and improved auditability. Measure review time, citation correctness, completeness, correction rate, escalation rate, and cost per case. Monitor unauthorized retrieval attempts, missing or stale evidence, transcription quality by accent or channel, retention execution, and model/provider changes.

Implementation and control points

Define the approved use, prohibited automated decisions, records schedule, and reviewer responsibilities before the pilot. Use tenant and case-level authorization during retrieval; show source quotations and time offsets; make uncertainty visible and allow abstention. Do not treat generated summaries as the official record until reviewed. Minimize sensitive prompt/response logging, restrict audit access, and test deletion and legal-hold behavior across transcripts, indexes, and caches.

Interview framing for architecture and engineering leadership

How do you choose a multimodal architecture?

I start by tracing one user task end to end and identifying which inputs actually need model interpretation. For example, I would compare rules or OCR for stable fields, speech-to-text for audio, a vision model for images, and a multimodal model only where cross-modal reasoning is necessary. I would run the candidates against a rights-cleared evaluation set and compare task-level accuracy, critical error types, latency, unit cost, and data-processing constraints. I would keep identity, authorization, workflow state, and final business decisions in ordinary services, then pilot behind an API with human review and a non-AI fallback.

Further reading: NIST Generative AI Profile; NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

How do you make media metadata trustworthy?

I define a stable asset ID and a schema with an owner for each field: source facts such as capture time remain distinct from model-generated suggestions such as topic or speaker. Every derived value carries source version, processing time, model/prompt version, confidence where supported, and review status. Ingestion validates required fields and vocabulary; consumers filter on authoritative rights and access fields, never on a model-generated label. I also make deletion a workflow across object storage, derivatives, search indexes, caches, and backups, with an auditable completion state.

Further reading: NIST AI Risk Management Framework; Azure AI Search security and role-based access control.

What does production-ready LLMOps mean?

I treat a release as a compatibility set: application build, model/provider version, prompt template, tool schemas, retrieval configuration/index snapshot, and evaluation-set version. CI runs deterministic tests plus task-quality, citation, access-control, injection, and cost/latency evaluations; a named owner reviews threshold failures rather than accepting a single aggregate score. I promote through a staging environment and limited canary, compare live service and quality signals, and retain a tested rollback or kill switch. In production I assign service and model owners, alert on availability, latency, queue depth, quality indicators, safety reports, and spend, and re-evaluate after any material dependency or data change.

Further reading: NIST Generative AI Profile; Azure Well-Architected Framework.

How do you lead adoption as a Director of Engineering?

I would put a business owner and an engineering owner on a bounded workflow, document the current process, and agree how we will measure value, quality, and risk before selecting a model. I staff a cross-functional delivery group—product, domain operations, data, platform, security, privacy/legal, and support—with explicit decision rights and an incident path. The team ships a limited pilot with evaluation gates, human override, cost limits, and operational support; we review adoption, correction rates, task outcomes, and total run cost with the business. I scale only when those results are repeatable and the platform, governance, and support model can absorb more users and data sources.

Further reading: NIST AI Risk Management Framework; Azure Well-Architected Framework.

OSI model, inbound/outbound traffic, and data protection

The Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model is a vocabulary for locating communication functions. It does not prescribe a secure cloud configuration. Apply controls at multiple layers and validate actual service behavior, routes, and endpoints.

Common AI-prone attacks and mitigations

Generative AI introduces untrusted natural-language inputs and probabilistic outputs into applications. Existing security controls remain essential, but model filters alone cannot reliably enforce authorization or prevent every attack.

Attack / risk How it occurs (high level) Safeguards and implementation
Direct and indirect prompt injection A user message or retrieved page/document contains instructions that try to redirect the model, expose context, or influence a connected tool. Separate instructions from quoted/untrusted content; minimize retrieved context; screen and label sources; constrain output and tools; require server-side authorization and human approval for consequential actions; test direct and retrieved-content cases.
Retrieval poisoning and stale knowledge Unreviewed, altered, or outdated material enters a search index and influences generated answers. Ingest only approved sources; record provenance, owner, version, and effective date; validate and scan content; apply document ACLs during retrieval; support rapid removal/re-indexing; evaluate citations and freshness.
Sensitive information disclosure Over-broad retrieval, unsafe prompts, verbose traces, or a misconfigured API returns confidential data to an unauthorized user or destination. Enforce authorization before retrieval and before response; minimize/ redact context; isolate tenants; restrict model destinations; protect logs; use data loss prevention where suitable; test for cross-user leakage and prompt/response retention.
Excessive agency and tool misuse A model-connected tool has broad permissions or accepts an unsafe request, causing an unintended external action. Use narrow typed tools and dedicated least-privilege identities; allowlist destinations and operations; validate arguments and business rules in code; make read-only the default; require explicit human approval for high-impact actions; log approvals.
Unsafe output handling Generated text is treated as trusted HTML, SQL, shell input, code, or a business decision without validation. Encode output for its display context; validate structured responses against a schema; use parameterized database operations; never execute generated code or commands directly; apply domain policy and human review before consequential use.
Denial of service and denial of wallet High-volume, oversized, repetitive, or deliberately expensive requests consume model capacity or incur excessive charges. Set per-user and global quotas, input/token limits, concurrency ceilings, deadlines, bounded retries, and spending alerts; use queue limits and safe degradation; protect public ingress with appropriate rate and abuse controls.
Model or dependency supply-chain compromise A vulnerable library, untrusted model artifact, compromised image, or unreviewed provider/configuration change enters the system. Use approved registries and model sources; scan dependencies and images; pin and verify versions/provenance; protect build/release identities; review provider terms and changes; evaluate updates and retain rollback options.
Model extraction and sensitive inference Repeated queries or responses reveal more about model behavior, training data, or sensitive inputs than intended. Minimize exposed endpoints and response detail; authenticate and rate-limit access; do not train on sensitive data without approval; review provider data handling; monitor unusual query patterns and protect evaluation/training sets.
Training or fine-tuning data poisoning Low-quality or malicious examples contaminate a training/fine-tuning corpus and bias model behavior. Use curated, traceable datasets; validate provenance and quality; separate training from production; review data changes; evaluate for targeted regressions and keep reproducible dataset/model versions. If using a hosted foundation model without custom training, focus on retrieval/index integrity instead.
Unsafe third-party tools and SSRF A model-directed integration fetches an untrusted URL or reaches a resource outside its intended boundary. Avoid arbitrary URL-fetch tools; allowlist destinations; validate resolved addresses and redirects; block access to metadata and internal networks unless explicitly required; use controlled egress and test DNS/rebinding edge cases with authorized security testing.

Inbound traffic (into the workload)

  • Expose only the intended entry point. Prefer private ingress for internal applications; for public apps, use a managed edge with WAF, denial-of-service protection, and rate controls.
  • Require strong authentication, short-lived tokens, audience/issuer checks, authorization at each API/action, and request-size and content-type limits.
  • Use TLS 1.2 or newer where supported by the service and client policy; prefer TLS 1.3 where compatible. Disable obsolete protocol versions and weak cipher suites.
  • Validate uploads, sanitize rendered output, prevent server-side request forgery (SSRF), and do not accept model-generated tool commands without schema and policy checks.

Outbound traffic (from the workload)

  • Inventory required dependencies and allow only necessary destinations; route through a controlled egress point when the platform supports it.
  • Use private endpoints for supported model, search, storage, and secrets services where appropriate. Disable public network access where feasible and test DNS/private routing.
  • Prevent arbitrary URLs, open proxies, and unrestricted model tools. Validate destination hostnames and block metadata-service and internal address targets unless explicitly needed.
  • Use quotas, timeouts, retries with caps, and egress monitoring. A private endpoint is not itself authentication or authorization.

Data at rest (stored data)

  • Encrypt databases, object storage, disks, backups, logs, indexes, caches, and model artifacts using service encryption; consider customer-managed keys (CMK) where policy or threat model calls for control over key lifecycle.
  • Keep keys and secrets in a managed vault with managed identity, least privilege, rotation, access auditing, and recovery protections. Never place secrets in source code, prompts, images, or logs.
  • Classify and minimize data; set retention, deletion, backup, residency, and legal-hold policies. Treat embeddings and indexes as sensitive: they can reveal information about source data.
  • Separate tenant data and access boundaries; test deletion and restore workflows.

Data in transit (moving data)

  • Use TLS for clients, APIs, service-to-service calls, and model endpoints. Validate certificates; use mutual TLS (mTLS) when workload identity and threat model justify it.
  • Encrypt traffic over private networks too. Private routing limits exposure but does not make data confidential or authenticate a caller.
  • Protect data copied into prompts, retrieval results, traces, queues, and telemetry. Avoid recording raw prompts/responses by default; redact or tokenize sensitive fields.
  • Use managed identity or short-lived credentials for service access rather than embedded keys. Document every external model/API destination and its data terms.
Encryption is not access control: encryption at rest and in transit reduces exposure if storage or a network path is accessed improperly, but it does not prevent an authorized yet over-privileged app, user, or model workflow from reading or disclosing data. Combine it with authorization, minimization, auditing, and lifecycle controls.

  • Use managed identities, Key Vault integration, protected container registries, signed/scanned images, approved base images, admission policy, and patch/upgrade ownership.

AI safety and data boundaries

  • Enforce role-, claim-, and document-level permissions before retrieval; filter results in the data service and test for cross-member and cross-tenant leakage.
  • Retrieve only the minimum necessary claim and policy data. Treat documents as untrusted content: detect malicious instruction patterns, do not allow documents to grant tool permissions, and cite the source passages.
  • Require adjuster review for decisions or communications affecting eligibility, payment, coverage, or care. Show uncertainty, sources, limitations, and an escalation/appeal path.
  • Encrypt persistent claim data, indexes, backups, and logs; keep sensitive health information out of telemetry and prompts unless approved and necessary. Define retention and deletion across source, index, cache, and logs.

Production controls and measures

  • Use a documented evaluation set with privacy-approved cases; test groundedness, citation correctness, unsafe advice, subgroup performance, prompt injection, and unauthorized retrieval before release.
  • Pin model and prompt versions, stage rollout, set concurrency and token budgets, monitor latency, errors, drift and safety escalations, and retain a deterministic manual-review fallback.
  • Audit access and material actions. Store correlation IDs and decision evidence while minimizing or redacting raw prompts/responses.
  • Success measures: lower review preparation time without increasing unsupported summaries, privacy incidents, inappropriate recommendations, or inequitable outcomes.

Threats addressed

Cross-tenant data exposure, poisoned or malicious claim documents, prompt injection, compromised container images, excessive service permissions, exposed Kubernetes management, and model output presented as authoritative medical or coverage advice.

Security and network design

  • Use a workload-profiles ACA environment integrated with a controlled virtual network where private connectivity, user-defined routes, or custom egress are required. Make ingress internal for private users; expose only a protected gateway if external access is needed.
  • Apply environment-level network restrictions and an egress allowlist for approved model, registry, secrets, telemetry, and business API dependencies. Keep the AI environment segmented from OT networks.
  • Use managed identities for secrets and service access; use private endpoints and private DNS for supported dependencies. Do not hard-code credentials in images or application configuration; use platform-managed secret references where needed.
  • Use immutable, scanned images, trusted registries, least-privilege container settings, resource limits, revision-based rollout, and controlled secrets/configuration updates.

AI safety and action boundaries

  • Separate read-only retrieval from the draft-work-order capability. The draft tool accepts typed fields from an allowlist; it cannot change equipment state, safety interlocks, or control systems.
  • Require engineer confirmation and existing work-management authorization before submission. Record the human approver and retain the original sources and generated draft for audit.
  • Label maintenance records and manuals as untrusted context. Exclude commands embedded in retrieved content from tool instructions; require citations and show uncertainty for conflicting or stale procedures.
  • Maintain a safety-reviewed, version-controlled corpus and site/equipment access filters. Do not retrieve sensitive site details for users lacking the required authorization.

Production controls and measures

  • Use ACA scaling based on measured queue/concurrency needs with hard maximum replicas, model concurrency limits, token budgets, and spend alerts to prevent runaway consumption.
  • Use separate revisions or environments for test and production; validate prompt injection, hallucinated procedures, access filtering, and fail-safe behavior before promoting changes.
  • Monitor request traces, dependency health, retrieval freshness, safety reports, and unusual egress. Avoid logging sensitive operational content unless retention and access are approved.
  • Success measures: faster manual lookup and better-documented draft work orders without bypassing work permits, human approval, operational safety procedures, or OT boundaries.

Threats addressed

Untrusted field notes steering the assistant, unauthorized disclosure of site information, a model-triggered unsafe action, compromised container revisions, and unbounded model traffic or spend.

Production release and operations checklist

Before release

  • Document intended use, prohibited use, users, data types, model/provider, dependencies, trust boundaries, and accountable risk owner.
  • Threat-model OWASP LLM risks and ordinary application/cloud threats. Perform privacy, safety, and regulatory reviews appropriate to the domain.
  • Test authorization bypass, cross-tenant retrieval, prompt injection, sensitive-data leakage, unsafe output rendering, tool misuse, rate-limit bypass, and denial-of-service/cost abuse.
  • Run quality and safety evaluations on representative and adversarial cases; define measurable go/no-go thresholds and known limitations.
  • Verify encryption, private routing, key/secret rotation, backup restore, monitoring, incident response, and rollback.

After release

  • Monitor model quality, access decisions, errors, latency, token usage, costs, and safety incidents; route incidents to named owners.
  • Re-evaluate after changes to models, prompts, tools, indexes, data sources, dependencies, and deployment configuration.
  • Review alerts and audit events for misuse while minimizing access to sensitive prompt and response content.
  • Provide user reporting, human escalation, service disablement, and a tested lower-risk fallback.
  • Periodically reassess whether the system remains necessary, proportionate, accurate enough, and within its approved scope.

25 interview questions with sample answers

These examples demonstrate how to explain a design, its control points, and how it is operated. Adapt the technologies and detail to the role and to work you have actually delivered; do not present illustrative approaches or metrics as personal accomplishments.

1. How do you distinguish AI safety from AI security?

I treat them as related but separate risk tracks. For safety, I define the intended and prohibited uses, affected users, harm scenarios, human review points, and measurable evaluation cases; for security, I map identities, data flows, trust boundaries, tools, and abuse paths. Those tracks meet in the release gate: for example, an answer may be factually useful but still unsafe if it exposes another tenant's record. I assign owners, tests, escalation procedures, and rollback criteria to both rather than relying on a model refusal.

Further reading: NIST AI Risk Management Framework; NIST Generative AI Profile.

2. How do you threat-model a retrieval-augmented generation system?

I draw the flow from authenticated user through API, authorization, query rewriting, retrieval, prompt assembly, model, tools, response rendering, and telemetry. At each boundary I ask what identity and data are trusted, then test cases such as a user requesting another tenant's document, a poisoned document containing instructions, unsafe rendered output, an overprivileged tool, and a burst of expensive requests. Controls include authorization filters in the retrieval service, untrusted-context handling, typed and scoped tools, output validation, quotas, and minimized logs. I turn each risk into a test with an owner and verify both the expected allow path and the deny path before release.

Further reading: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications; OWASP ASVS.

3. What is prompt injection, and how do you mitigate it?

Prompt injection is untrusted user or retrieved content attempting to steer model behavior, including instructions hidden in documents or media transcripts. I assume prompts alone cannot reliably separate instructions from data: the application authorizes each retrieval independently, labels and bounds context, and never grants a tool broader authority because the model asks for it. Tool calls use allowlisted operations, typed arguments, server-side validation, and confirmation for consequential actions. I add direct and indirect injection examples to regression tests and alert on suspicious tool attempts, while recognizing that these controls reduce risk rather than proving the model immune.

Further reading: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications; OWASP ASVS.

4. Why is a system prompt not an authorization control?

A prompt is model input, not a cryptographic identity, policy decision, or database constraint. The caller's verified identity and entitlements must reach the API through a trusted channel; the service checks permission before retrieval and again before any write or tool action. I keep tenant and resource scopes in server-side query predicates and avoid accepting client-supplied tenant IDs as authority. I test with users who should be denied, including cache and export paths, because a polished refusal cannot undo data already returned to the model.

Further reading: OWASP ASVS; Azure AI Search security and role-based access control.

5. How do you prevent cross-tenant data leakage in RAG?

I derive the tenant and user from a validated identity token, then resolve current entitlements in a trusted service rather than trusting request parameters. Retrieval applies tenant and document ACL filters before ranking results or constructing model context; if the search platform cannot enforce that filter, I partition indexes or add a permission-checking layer before content leaves the data boundary. Cache keys include the effective authorization scope and relevant index version, and cached content is invalidated on ACL changes. I test adversarial cross-tenant queries, logs, exports, background jobs, and deletion, not just the happy-path API call.

Further reading: Azure AI Search security and role-based access control; OWASP ASVS.

6. How would you secure LLM tool or agent access?

I start with explicit business operations and expose narrow tools rather than a general shell, arbitrary URL fetch, or unrestricted database interface. Each tool has a schema, input bounds, authorization check, timeout, idempotency behavior, and a workload identity limited to its one downstream capability. The orchestrator treats model output as a proposal; the API independently validates the caller, current state, and business rules before execution. For irreversible or high-impact operations I show the proposed change and evidence to an authorized human, record approval and result, and provide a disable switch.

Further reading: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications; Azure managed identities overview.

7. How do you reduce hallucinations in a high-stakes workflow?

I first narrow the task and retrieve only current, approved sources the user is allowed to see. The response contract requires source references, and a post-processing step checks that cited IDs were actually retrieved and can be opened by that user; unsupported or conflicting evidence produces an explicit abstention or escalation. I evaluate answer correctness, citation support, omission, and appropriate abstention on representative and difficult cases, then review errors with domain experts. In consequential workflows the output is a draft or recommendation for a qualified reviewer, not an autonomous decision.

Further reading: NIST Generative AI Profile; NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

8. What does human-in-the-loop mean in practice?

I make the review step an explicit workflow state, not a note in the prompt. The reviewer sees the original evidence, citations, confidence or known limitations, the proposed action, and controls to edit, reject, or escalate; an API requires an authorized reviewer identity before the state can advance. I record what model/configuration produced the draft, who approved or changed it, and the resulting action under the business record's retention policy. I also test queue aging, reviewer availability, override rates, and the fail-closed behavior so automation cannot silently skip review when the service is busy.

Further reading: NIST AI Risk Management Framework; NIST Generative AI Profile.

9. How do you handle sensitive data in prompts and responses?

I classify the source data and define which fields and purposes are approved for model processing before integrating a provider. Retrieval returns the smallest authorized context; where possible, the application redacts or tokenizes identifiers before inference and reattaches them only in a controlled service. I verify provider data-use, region, retention, and access terms, and configure telemetry to capture IDs, versions, timings, and outcomes rather than raw prompts by default. Access to any retained content is restricted and audited, with retention, deletion, legal hold, and incident procedures covering traces, caches, indexes, and backups.

Further reading: Azure OpenAI data, privacy, and security; NIST Privacy Framework.

10. What is your approach to data at rest and in transit?

I inventory every copy of data—original assets, extracted text, vector indexes, queues, traces, backups, and model artifacts—then select encryption and key controls based on classification and service capability. Service-to-service calls use authenticated TLS, preferably private connectivity where the service and threat model support it; DNS, routes, and outbound destinations are verified in deployment tests. Managed identities avoid embedded credentials, and key access, rotation, recovery, and revocation have named owners. Encryption is one layer: I still enforce authorization, minimize retained content, and test deletion and restore behavior.

Further reading: Azure encryption overview; Azure network segmentation guidance.

11. How would you design inbound and outbound network security?

I document the required flows first: users to the API, API to model and search, workers to storage/queue, and telemetry to the monitoring service. Inbound access goes through the intended gateway and identity enforcement; internal services are not exposed just because they have an endpoint. Outbound access is limited to required destinations using the controls supported by the selected hosting plan, with private endpoints where applicable and explicit DNS and route design. I validate connectivity and denied flows from the deployed workload, because a subnet diagram alone does not prove effective isolation.

Further reading: Azure network segmentation guidance; Azure Container Apps networking.

12. How do you use the OSI model in a security review?

I use OSI as a shared vocabulary to locate controls, not as the architecture itself. For example, I discuss name resolution and routing at network layers, TLS and service identity around the transport/session boundary, and authentication, authorization, request validation, and safe rendering at the application layer. Then I map those controls to actual cloud resources and verify the end-to-end path, including identity and data permissions that OSI does not describe. That keeps the review practical without suggesting that a firewall solves application or AI-specific risks.

Further reading: ISO/IEC 7498-1: OSI Basic Reference Model; OWASP ASVS.

13. What OWASP guidance is relevant to LLM applications?

I use the current OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications as a threat discovery aid and ASVS for conventional application controls, then scope both to the actual workflow. For each relevant risk—such as prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, unsafe output handling, excessive agency, or unbounded consumption—I identify a control owner and a test at the relevant boundary. For example, cross-tenant retrieval becomes a negative authorization test, while excessive agency becomes a tool-scope and approval test. I track findings through remediation and regression tests rather than claiming a checklist alone establishes security.

Further reading: OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications; OWASP ASVS.

14. How do you evaluate an AI system before production?

I translate the intended use into an evaluation plan before choosing a model: representative tasks, edge cases, prohibited outcomes, risk owners, and measurable acceptance criteria. The test set includes task quality and failure categories, retrieval/citation support, refusal or escalation, privacy and authorization negatives, adversarial inputs, plus latency and cost under expected load. I keep a versioned baseline and run the same suite when the prompt, model, index, tool schema, or provider changes; domain reviewers inspect failures that automated metrics cannot judge. Promotion requires thresholds to pass, limitations and support readiness to be documented, and the accountable business and risk owners to approve.

Further reading: NIST Generative AI Profile; NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

15. What should be monitored after deployment?

I monitor conventional service signals—availability, latency by dependency, error rate, queue lag, saturation, and recovery—alongside model calls, token use, and spend by approved dimensions. Quality signals include sampled citation support, task completion or correction rate, abstention/escalation, and user reports; they need privacy controls and a review process because user feedback can be noisy. I alert on actionable thresholds such as a provider error spike, stale index, unexpected cost growth, or access-control anomaly, and route each to a named runbook owner. I avoid retaining raw prompts by default and verify that logs cannot become a secondary store of sensitive data.

Further reading: Azure Monitor overview; NIST Generative AI Profile.

16. How do you protect an AI supply chain?

I maintain an inventory of model/provider versions, datasets, prompts, evaluation assets, packages, base images, and external services, with provenance and accountable owners. CI scans dependencies and images, applies policy checks, and publishes immutable artifacts to a controlled registry; production identities can deploy approved artifacts but cannot silently replace them. Model and prompt changes go through the same review and evaluation process as code, with pinned versions where supported. I preserve release records and a tested rollback or disable path, and define how to respond if a provider, package, dataset, or model is withdrawn or compromised.

Further reading: NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0; AKS security concepts and supply-chain controls.

17. How would you secure an AI workload on AKS?

I begin with the cluster threat model and decide whether AKS is justified by requirements such as specialized scheduling, portability, or platform control; I avoid Kubernetes if a managed app service meets the need. On AKS I use managed identities and least-privilege Kubernetes/cloud RBAC, separate workloads and secrets by namespace and service account, and restrict ingress and egress with supported network policy and routing controls. The delivery pipeline builds and scans immutable images, enforces admission policy, and separates deploy permissions from runtime permissions. I monitor control-plane and workload health, patch nodes and dependencies, test restore and upgrade procedures, and include model/API quotas so autoscaling cannot create unbounded spend.

Further reading: AKS security concepts; Secure container access with Microsoft Entra workload ID.

18. When would you choose Azure App Service for an AI application?

I would choose App Service for a conventional web/API tier when its deployment model, scaling, runtime support, and networking fit the workload, while keeping model and data services behind explicit service interfaces. I would use managed identity for supported dependencies, store secrets in a managed secret service, and configure inbound access and outbound/private connectivity according to the selected plan's actual capabilities. I verify those plan-specific networking constraints before committing the design, rather than assuming every tier supports the same controls. Deployment slots or staged rollout, health checks, dependency timeouts, and a model-provider fallback can reduce release and availability risk.

Further reading: Azure App Service security; App Service virtual network integration.

19. When is Azure Container Apps a suitable choice?

I consider Container Apps for containerized APIs, asynchronous media/model workers, and event-driven jobs when I want managed container operations without operating a full Kubernetes platform. I separate public or internal ingress from background workers, give each app a distinct identity, and set scale rules, maximum replicas, concurrency, and queue back-pressure from measured workload behavior. I validate environment type, VNet integration, private endpoints, DNS, and outbound routing against the required threat model and Azure's current service capabilities. Revisions support controlled promotion, but I still test rollback, worker idempotency, and whether scale-out respects provider quotas and budget limits.

Further reading: Azure Container Apps networking; Azure Container Apps scaling.

20. How do you control model cost and denial-of-wallet risk?

I model cost per business transaction from expected input/output tokens, modality processing, retrieval, retries, and peak concurrency, then set per-user and global request/token limits. The gateway or application enforces payload limits, timeouts, concurrency, quotas, and bounded retries; retries must not amplify a provider outage. I use caching only where authorization scope, data freshness, and privacy make it safe, and alert on spend rate and unusual usage before a monthly budget is exhausted. Load tests verify both peak cost and graceful behavior at quota or provider-capacity limits, including a lower-cost or human fallback where appropriate.

Further reading: Azure Cost Management overview; OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications.

21. How do you respond to an AI safety incident?

I use the established incident process and first contain the specific risk: disable a tool, pause a model route, restrict a source/index, or take the feature offline while preserving necessary evidence. The incident lead brings in security, privacy, product, legal/compliance, and domain safety owners to determine affected users, data, decisions, and reporting obligations. We trace the release versions and relevant access/audit events, avoid unnecessarily collecting more sensitive content, and communicate through approved channels. Before restoring service, we fix the root cause, add a regression test, rerun the relevant evaluation, obtain risk-owner approval, and document follow-up actions.

Further reading: NIST AI Risk Management Framework; NIST Generative AI Profile.

22. How do you safely update a model or prompt?

I create an explicit change record covering model/provider version, prompt, tools, retrieval settings, index/data snapshot, application build, and the reason for change. I replay a fixed, access-controlled evaluation suite and compare task quality, safety, citation behavior, latency, and cost with the current baseline; domain reviewers inspect material regressions. After approval I promote to staging and a limited canary, monitoring both service health and sampled outcome signals. The previous known-good configuration remains deployable, and the operator can roll back or disable the affected capability without waiting for a code release.

Further reading: NIST Generative AI Profile; Azure Well-Architected Framework.

23. What is a safe fallback when an AI dependency fails?

I set a deadline for the end-to-end request, use bounded retries with backoff only for retryable failures, and open a circuit breaker when the dependency is unhealthy. The fallback is designed per task: approved search, a queued request, human handling, or a clear unavailable response—not a silent switch to an untested model. Authorization and safety gates remain in force in every mode, and any cached answer is checked for freshness and current permissions. I exercise the failure path in integration tests and operational game days, including recovery and queue-drain behavior.

Further reading: Azure Well-Architected reliability principles; NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

24. How do you address fairness and explainability concerns?

I first identify who may be affected and whether the model's output could influence access, service, employment, safety, or another consequential outcome. With domain and legal experts, I define appropriate subgroup and error analyses, check whether the evaluation data represents real operating conditions, and investigate differences rather than treating one aggregate score as proof of fairness. For generative answers I provide source evidence, model/configuration traceability, known limitations, and a way to challenge or correct the result; explanation must not imply certainty the model does not have. If the use cannot be adequately evaluated or governed, I narrow or reject the use case.

Further reading: NIST AI Risk Management Framework; NIST Generative AI Profile.

25. How do you decide whether an AI solution is ready for production?

I use a readiness review with evidence, not a demo: a named business owner, approved intended use, data rights, threat model, architecture and support owners, and a measured baseline/business case. The team demonstrates passing quality and safety evaluations, authorization and privacy tests, capacity and cost limits, dependency failure behavior, observability, incident runbooks, and recovery/rollback. We also verify user guidance, human escalation, retention, and who can disable the feature. Launch is a risk-owner decision with staged exposure and explicit stop criteria; unresolved risks are documented and accepted by the appropriate authority, not hidden in a project plan.

Further reading: Azure Well-Architected Framework; NIST Generative AI Profile.
